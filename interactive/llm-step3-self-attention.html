Self-attention: letting tokens look at each other

This is the mechanism that made Transformers work. Each token asks a question (Query), every token advertises what it has (Key), and the matches decide how much each token's content (Value) flows into the result. We follow one sentence through the whole series — "The king sat on the ___" — and watch the last word reach back to "king" to work out what comes next.

00The Problem First

Why not just use the embeddings directly?

After Step 2 we have X, one vector per token of "The king sat on the ___". To predict the next word we use the last vector — but the last "the" is a function word. Its vector says almost nothing about who sat or on what. It must pull that information in from the other tokens.

X = embedding E + position P — the input to attention (5 tokens × 7 numbers)

Each row is one token: its word embedding (Step 1) plus a small position stamp (Step 2, scaled to 0.3). Attention will turn X into three matrices: Q = X·Wq, K = X·Wk, V = X·Wv (each W is 7×3, giving 5×3). The next card shows Q and K.

Attempt 1 — compare the embeddings themselves

The obvious plan: take the last row of X and take its dot product with every row (a dot product is just "multiply matching numbers and add them up" — big when two vectors overlap). Then look mostly at the tokens that score highest. No Q, K or V needed. The last column of the table below shows what that does.

Attempt 2 — give every token a question and an advertisement

Instead of comparing whole embeddings, the model makes two short vectors from each row of X:

  • Query (Q) — "what am I looking for?"
  • Key (K) — "what do I advertise about myself?"

Both are made from X by multiplying with a learned matrix (Q = X·Wq, K = X·Wk). Here we shrink each token to just 3 numbers and — only so you can read them — we label the three numbers [noun-ness, action-ness, royalty]. (In a real model the numbers are learned and have no human labels; training discovers whatever features make good matches. Here we use matrices solved to make the three numbers readable.)

Where Q and K come from: multiply X by a learned matrix

Each token's row of X (7 numbers) is multiplied by a 7×3 matrix to get its 3-number Query, and by a different 7×3 matrix to get its 3-number Key. Row by row, this is just 7 multiply-and-adds per output number:

Honest note: in a real model Wq and Wk start random and are learned in training (Step 5), and their three output columns have no human names. Here we solved for matrices that make the three columns come out as [noun-ness, action-ness, royalty] so you can read them. The multiplication is exactly the real thing. (The weights are biggish, up to about ±8, because the two "the" rows differ only by that small position nudge and we asked for very different Queries.)

Reading the table: how the last "the" decides where to look

  1. The last "the" writes down its Query. Below it is Q ≈ [0.6, 1.4, 2.0] (computed as X·Wq, above): it wants a bit of noun, some action, and above all royalty.
  2. Each token shows its Key — e.g. "king" advertises [1, 0, 1] (a noun, royal); "sat" advertises [0, 1, 0] (an action).
  3. Score = Q·K: multiply feature by feature and add. For "king": 0.6×1 + 1.4×0 + 2.0×1 = 2.6. For "sat": 1.4. For the two "the"s: 0, because they advertise nothing. We then divide by √3 (a standard scaling to keep the numbers small; explained below).
  4. Softmax turns the scores into percentages that add to 100% — the attention weights.

Same word, two different jobs: the first "The" vs. the last "the"

Our sentence contains "the" twice. In this toy both get the same embedding (a real tokenizer would give "The" and " the" different IDs), so only their positions differ. That alone is enough to give them different Queries, and they behave very differently. The mask (a token may only look at itself and earlier tokens) is switched on here, as in a real generating model:

What goes wrong — and what Q, K, V add

1 · Similar is not the same as relevant. Embeddings put alike words close together (king ≈ queen). But what the last "the" needs is a different kind of word — a royal subject and a sitting verb. Raw similarity makes the last "the" attend mostly to itself, and gives "king" the least. Q and K are learned lenses that turn "what I need" and "what I offer" into vectors that line up even when the words are unalike.
2 · The relationship is one-way. Xthe·Xking equals Xking·Xthe, so raw similarity is symmetric. But "the" needs "king", not the other way round. Separate Q and K matrices break the symmetry: each word can ask one thing and advertise another.
3 · One vector can't do three jobs. The embedding has to say who the word is, how to match it, and what to pass on. Splitting it gives each job its own copy: Q = what I'm looking for, K = what I advertise for matching, V = what I hand over once matched. Blending raw embeddings would copy all of "king" wholesale; V lets the model pass on just the useful part.

So Q, K, V are the same X seen through three different learned lenses (Wq, Wk, Wv). The embedding says what a word is; Q, K and V say what it needs, what it offers, and what it delivers.

01The Three Roles

Q asks, K offers, V delivers

You have just seen the idea at work: a compatibility score (a bit like a correlation) between what one token needs and what another advertises (Q·K) decides where to look, and the content of what it finds (V) is blended in to make the embedding better. The three vectors, in one place:

Q Query — what this token is looking for
e.g. the last "the" looking for who is sitting, and on what K Key — what this token advertises about itself
e.g. "king" advertising: noun, royal, subject V Value — the actual content that flows when matched
e.g. the royal-ness payload the last "the" receives from "king"

Q asks · K offers · V delivers. Score (Q·K) decides how much to attend; V is what actually flows. You never receive the Key — it's just the index.

One common trip-up: V is not the token's original embedding. Q, K, and V are three separate learned projections of the same input vector — V is a dedicated "here's what I hand over if you attend to me" copy, kept apart from the routing done by Q and K.

In the model: Q, K, and V are each the token's vector X multiplied by a learned weight matrix (W_Q, W_K, W_V) — you saw Q = X·Wq and K = X·Wk computed above. In a real model those matrices are learned from data, exactly like the embeddings in Step 1.

03Two Different Jobs

Q·K decides who to listen to — V decides what you hear

It's easy to blur Q, K and V together. They do two completely separate jobs, and keeping them apart is the whole trick of attention.

1 · Q · K — a relevance (compatibility) score between two words

The dot product of one word's Query with another word's Key measures how well they match: large when the two vectors point the same way, near zero when unrelated, negative when opposed. Think of it as a compatibility score, a bit like a correlation (but not normalised to −1…1), between "what I'm looking for" and "what you advertise". Do this for every pair and you get the attention matrix above. No word content moves yet — this step only produces scores (then softmax turns them into weights that sum to 1).

2 · V — the information that actually gets passed along

A word's Value is its payload: the content it hands over if someone attends to it. Q and K are only used to match; V is what is delivered. That's why K and V are separate projections: how a word is found and what it gives once found are different things.

Three roles in our sentence  ·  Q (last "the") = "who sat, and on what?"  ·  K ("king") = "I'm a royal noun, the subject"  ·  V ("king") = "here's what 'king' means"

3 · How attention improves the embedding that went in

Go back to the problem: the last "the" went into attention with an almost empty vector (it only knows "I am a function word"). Q·Kᵀ told us where to look (mostly "king"). Now we use those weights to blend the other tokens' Values, and the blend is added to the vector. Below, every number is computed live. For readability we let each token's Value be its own content features (the 7 numbers from Step 2); in a real model V is a learned projection.

The weights come from Q·K; the things being mixed are Vs. So the last "the" started as a nearly empty function-word vector, and comes out as a vector that is mostly "king" with some "sat on" mixed in — it has absorbed context, and is now a good basis for predicting "throne". Every word does this simultaneously, so after one attention layer each embedding is a context-aware blend of the others, not just itself. Stack layers and the mixing compounds: words gather information from words that already gathered information.

Why three vectors instead of just comparing embeddings? Because attention is asymmetric: the last "the" is looking for "king", but "king" isn't looking for a following "the". Q and K are two different learned views of the same embedding, so each word can ask one thing and advertise another. (Here the vectors are hand-made; in a real model the projection matrices Wq, Wk, Wv are learned in training — Step 5.) Everything on this page is one head in one layer; Step 4 repeats it side by side (heads) and stacked (layers).

Two consequences worth remembering: (a) a word that nobody attends to still keeps its own value through the residual connection (Step 4); (b) in generation, the causal mask means a word can only be changed by words before it — which is exactly why old K and V never change and can be cached in Step 7.

4 · What are Wq, Wk and Wv each learning?

All three multiply the same X, so what makes them different is the feedback each one gets in training (Step 5).

  • Wq learns what each kind of word should look for. If the last "the" put its attention on "on" when "king" would have predicted "throne" better, the loss goes up and Wq is nudged so that Query points more towards royal nouns.
  • Wk learns what each kind of word should advertise. It is trained together with Wq: only the dot product Q·K ever reaches the loss, so the two matrices are optimised jointly to give useful scores. Neither means much alone.
  • Wv learns which parts of a word are worth copying, and how much of each. A weight near 0 leaves that part of the word behind, a large weight carries it strongly, and a negative weight flips it. It usually also narrows the vector (here 7 numbers → 3; in Llama-3 8B each head turns the 4096-number hidden vector into 128 numbers, and there are many heads), so it has to choose what to keep.

Why the feedback differs. Q and K only decide the attention weights, so they learn from "did I look at the right words?". V is the content that actually gets copied, so it learns from "did the copied content help predict the next word?". If a copied feature made "throne" more likely, its weight grows. If it was noise, its weight shrinks.

In card 3 above, each word handed over all of its features (every dial at 1). Below you set how much of each feature is passed on. The weights from Q·K stay the same; only what is copied changes. Watch the next-word guess.

One dial per feature is its simplest form (a matrix with numbers only on its diagonal). Training finds the dial settings, or the full matrix, that lower the loss.

04The Pipeline

Score → Scale → Soften → Blend → Write back

Attention proper is four operations — the ones inside the softmax(QKT/√dk)V formula. You already saw them in the step-by-step calc above. A fifth step, the output projection, runs just after — it lives in the multi-head wrapper, not the formula itself, so we show it set apart:

1
Score Q · K
Dot-product of the query with every key. A high score means "this token is relevant to my question." Scores are unbounded — any positive or negative number.
↓
2
Scale ÷ √dk
Divide by the square root of the key dimension. Without this, larger embedding sizes produce bigger dot products, pushing softmax into near-zero gradients and making training stall.
why square root specifically?

If each element of Q and K is drawn from a distribution with variance 1, their dot product has variance dk. Dividing by √dk brings the variance back to 1 regardless of dimension size.

↓
3
Soften softmax
Converts raw scores into weights that sum to 1 — a probability distribution over which tokens to attend to. Higher scores get exponentially more weight; low scores shrink toward zero.
↓
4
Blend × V
Weighted average of all value vectors. Tokens with high attention weight contribute more of their content. The result is a single context-aware vector for this query token.
⋮
5
Write back · WO → + residual the wrapper step — outside the formula
The blended vector isn't the output yet. With multiple heads, each head's blend is concatenated, then a final learned matrix WO mixes them and projects the result back into the model's working dimension. That result is added onto the token's own representation — the residual connection you'll meet in Step 4. In the mnemonic: O writes back.
05The Whole Thing

One formula, four stages

The four stages collapse into a single expression you'll see in every Transformer paper:

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( QKT / √dk ) V
QKT = score every key against every query · /√dk = scale · softmax = soften into weights · × V = blend values
In a causal decoder a mask M (−∞ for future positions) is added before the softmax, so the full form is softmax(QKT/√dk + M)V, with the softmax taken across the keys for each query row. Real implementations use batched tensors such as [batch, sequence, heads, head_dim]; the 2D matrices here are the toy version.
The formula stops at V. That's the four stages above — but remember the fifth, write-back (WO), runs just after and merges the result into the token's residual stream. The whole mechanic in one line:
Q asks · K offers · V delivers · O writes back
This is self-attention because Q, K, and V all come from the same sequence — tokens attend to each other.
cross-attention (used in translation models)

In encoder-decoder models like the original Transformer, the decoder draws Q from its own tokens but K and V from the encoder's output. The math is identical — only the source of K/V changes.

Where this is heading

One attention pattern only captures one kind of relationship. Real models run many attention heads in parallel — one might track subjects, another track position, another track tone — then combine them. That, plus the feed-forward layers and residual connections that wrap around attention, is the full Transformer block. That's Step 4.
